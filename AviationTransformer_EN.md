## Abstract

## Introduction
[Broad Context]
Aviation meteorology is a core element in ensuring flight safety and improving operational efficiency. How meteorological information is processed, interpreted, summarized, and applied to decision-making across different flight phases has a direct impact on the overall performance of the air transportation system. Traditional aviation weather services rely heavily on highly encoded alphanumeric formats (such as METAR/SPECI and TAF) and fragmented, unstructured textual sources (such as PIREPs, NOTAMs, and Area Forecast Discussions). While these formats are extremely efficient for transmission over bandwidth-limited aviation communication systems, they place substantial demands on pilots’ ability to decode, integrate, and act on information in real time. With rapid advances in modern meteorological observation technologies, aviation weather data has shifted from scarcity to overload. This growing “cognitive burden” has become a key bottleneck limiting effective and timely decision-making.

[Specific Focus]
Compared with commercial pilots, who typically undergo longer training and operate in more standardized and tightly controlled airspace, general aviation (GA) pilots often fly in less predictable environments, frequently within uncontrolled airspace (Class G) and with limited or no air traffic control (ATC) support. As a result, GA pilots have a more urgent need for intelligent systems that can organize information and support decision-making in a clear and reliable manner.

[The Problem]
In recent years, large language models (LLMs) based on the Transformer architecture have emerged as a promising foundation for building such intelligent systems. However, decoder-only models, despite their strong generalization capabilities, suffer from several notable limitations:
1. In highly specialized vertical domains, limited coverage in pre-training data leads to a significant risk of hallucination.
2. Due to the Byte Pair Encoding (BPE)–based token representation mechanism, numerical values in the input may be split in unintuitive ways, leading to unexpected results.
3. Models with strong reasoning capabilities typically require large parameter sizes, making them expensive and impractical for local deployment, which limits their applicability in real-world cockpit environments.

[Research Objective]
To address the issues outlined above, we build an intelligent system for organizing, analyzing, and translating aviation meteorological information, using a locally deployable 8B-parameter model as the core engine. The system takes aviation weather information along a given flight route (including METAR, TAF, and SIGMET) as input and produces a pilot-centric route weather briefing with a clear information hierarchy as output. The briefing is generated entirely in natural language and is suitable for both text display and voice delivery.
The system integrates a Graph-based Retrieval-Augmented Generation (Graph RAG) module to provide the agent with precise domain-specific knowledge. In addition, we adopt the principles of the Numeric Logic Tokenizer and introduce domain-aware data preprocessing and inductive bias injection to enhance the model’s ability to handle numerical information. Furthermore, we leverage data generated by a teacher model and apply the RALA-DPO (Retrieval-Augmented LLM Alignment via Direct Preference Optimization) method to fine-tune the 8B student model. This process improves domain-specific reasoning performance, particularly in critical weather scenarios that have historically contributed to aviation accidents.

[Contributions]
The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
1. We collect and organize publicly available key resources in the general aviation domain (including aviation weather reporting standards and operational manuals) and construct a knowledge graph database with a three-level community structure, supporting search methods such as beam search.
2. Inspired by the Numeric Logic Tokenizer framework, we design an effective data preprocessing strategy tailored to the numerical characteristics of aviation meteorology.
3. We develop a complete and transferable teacher–student model fine-tuning pipeline.
4. Using public data sources such as NTSB reports and NWS Area Forecast Discussions, we curate and release an open-source training dataset focused on critical meteorological scenarios to facilitate future research.

[Thesis Outline]
The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Chapter 2 reviews related work on the application of LLMs in aviation meteorology. Chapter 3 presents the overall system architecture and hierarchical design, as well as the model fine-tuning pipeline. Chapter 4 describes the training dataset, metric selection, and evaluation methodology, reports baseline results, and compares them with the performance achieved by our proposed system. Finally, Chapter 5 concludes this work and outlines potential directions for future improvements.

## Related Work
To address the hallucination problem of large language models in specialized domains, the Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) framework proposed by Patrick Lewis et al. (Meta) has been widely adopted. For example, AviationGPT applies this approach to the aviation domain. By segmenting large volumes of domain-specific materials and converting them into vector representations, the model can retrieve relevant documents based on similarity to the input, thereby enriching its background knowledge. Building upon RAG, Darren Edge et al. (Microsoft) proposed GraphRAG, which further enhances the model’s global understanding of domain knowledge and enables the use of graph-based retrieval algorithms such as beam search.

In addition to retrieval augmentation, domain knowledge injection through model fine-tuning is another important approach to mitigating hallucinations. However, full-parameter fine-tuning is often costly and inefficient for large models. The LoRA method provides a solid foundation for low-cost and efficient fine-tuning, while QLoRA further reduces the computational and memory requirements. AviationGPT also adopts model fine-tuning techniques. Specifically, it collects large-scale unlabeled aviation-domain text, including accident reports, technical manuals, and weather briefings, and applies self-supervised learning to adapt the model to the aviation context. On this basis, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is performed using carefully constructed instruction–response pairs, enabling the model to understand specific task instructions. It is worth noting that directly fine-tuning models on highly encoded and compressed aviation data does not fully leverage the generalization capabilities learned from large-scale natural language corpora.

Numerical accuracy is a major challenge for generative language models when handling safety-critical data, and this issue is particularly prominent in aviation applications. In standard Transformer architectures, tokenizers often split numerical values into semantically meaningless fragments. The xVal approach attempts to address this issue by introducing a new numerical encoding scheme that injects numerical values directly into the embedding layer as continuous variables. While elegant in design, this method is better suited to architectures with absolute positional encoding. Since most existing large models rely on relative positional encodings such as RoPE, directly applying this approach becomes more challenging. In contrast, NumeroLogic proposes a non-intrusive and more general solution that operates purely at the input preprocessing stage. By formatting numerical values in a special way, it guides the model to better understand numerical precision and magnitude, producing an effect similar to chain-of-thought reasoning.

Building upon these prior studies, we introduce a rule-based decoder layer into our system to transform highly encoded aviation weather data into language chunks that can be more easily understood by general language models. We then apply Graph RAG to classify meteorological information according to severity and to provide corresponding operational recommendations. This process produces a structured intermediate report that has been preprocessed using NumeroLogic. The report is used as input to the model, which is trained through iterative self-supervised learning and fine-tuned using RALA-DPO. The final output is a route weather briefing that conforms to established aviation standards.
